First, a storyFirst, a story

At the end the author says he didn't write the book to tell you exactly what to buy, but to give you a way of looking at money. He admits it himself: the examples happened in America, decades ago, and both the rules and the markets have changed. The line he repeats is: 'I don't recommend a single thing I've written to you.'At the end the author says he did not write the book to tell you what to buy, but to give you a way of looking at money. He admits the examples happened in America decades ago, under different rules and markets. His repeated line: nothing I have written is a recommendation.

So we have to bring a critical eye too. Moves in the book like “a $60,000 house for $20,000” and “$190,000 from six trades” carry high leverage, a particular era, and a U.S. legal backdrop. Ordinary people copying them will very likely lose money. Learn the thinking, don't copy the operations—that's the single most important discipline of this course.So we must read critically too. The book's “buy a $60,000 house for $20,000” and “six deals, $190,000” moves carry high leverage, a specific era and American law. Copying them can lose money badly. Borrow the thinking, never copy the trades — that is this course's most important rule.

The book also ends with a preview from another book, Rich Dad's CASHFLOW Quadrant. It splits the ways people earn money into four quadrants: E Employee (work for others), S Self-employed (work for yourself), B Business owner (own a system), I Investor (let money work). The two on the left trade time for money; the two on the right let systems and money work for you.The closing pages also preview another book, Rich Dad's CASHFLOW Quadrant. It sorts how people earn into four quadrants: E employee (works for someone), S self-employed (works for themselves), B business owner (owns a system), I investor (money works). The left two trade time for money; the right two let systems and money work for them.

There's another story in the preview, told by the rich dad when the author was twelve. The village had no water, and the mayor contracted the water-carrying job to two people at once. Ed immediately bought two galvanized iron buckets and, before dawn each day, ran a mile to the lake to haul water and dump it into the village's concrete cistern. He made money, and he was happy.The preview also tells a story rich dad told the author at twelve. A village had no water, so the elders gave the daily water contract to two people at once. Ed immediately bought two galvanised steel buckets and ran to the lake a mile away before dawn each day, emptying them into the village's concrete tank. He made money and was happy.

Bill disappeared for a few months. Instead of buying buckets to compete, he wrote a business plan, registered a company, found four investors, hired a general manager, and came back six months later with a construction crew. Within a year, a stainless-steel pipeline connected the lake to the village. On the day the water flowed, he announced: cleaner water, 24 hours a day, at a price 75% below Ed's. The villagers cheered and ran toward the faucet at the end of the pipeline.Bill vanished for months. Instead of buying buckets to compete, he wrote a business plan, formed a corporation, found four investors, hired a president, and returned six months later with a construction crew. Within a year a stainless-steel pipeline linked the lake to the village. At the opening he announced cleaner water, twenty-four hours a day, at seventy-five percent less than Ed. The villagers cheered and ran to the tap at the end of the pipe.

Ed had to cut his price by 75%, bought two more buckets to carry four at a time, and hired his two sons to help. The sons later went to college and never came back. Bill, meanwhile, found that other villages were short of water too and sold the system around the world — earning just one cent a bucket but delivering billions of buckets a day. Whether he worked or not, the money kept flowing in.Ed had to cut his price by seventy-five percent, buy two more buckets, haul four at a time and hire his two sons. The sons went to college and never came back. Bill, meanwhile, saw that other villages needed water too, and sold the system worldwide — a penny per bucket, billions of buckets a day. Whether he worked or not, the money kept flowing in.

The author says he asked himself this story's question his whole life: "Am I building a pipeline or carrying buckets?" It's also the book's final question. You can carry buckets your whole life, or spend a stretch of time building a pipeline. Those months of building are dull and no one applauds—but once it's built, it keeps working while you sleep. Two roads: which one do you choose?The author says he has asked himself one question his whole life: am I building a pipeline, or hauling buckets? That is the book's final question too. You can haul buckets for a lifetime, or spend a while building a pipeline. Those months are dull and nobody applauds — but once it is built, it works while you sleep. Two roads. Which one do you take?
