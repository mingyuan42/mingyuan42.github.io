一个持续十年的赌局A Bet That Lasted a Decade

本课目标：弄清海龟实验到底在赌什么、两个人各自的立场，以及这场实验能证明什么、不能证明什么。

一、1983 年的一则小广告#

1983 年秋天，芝加哥最赚钱的期货交易员之一理查德·丹尼斯（Richard Dennis）在《华尔街日报》《巴伦周刊》和《国际先驱论坛报》上登了一则分类广告。广告很短，内容却反常：

招聘商品期货交易员。公司将培训一小批申请者，教授专有交易方法。被录用者只为公司交易，不得为自己或他人交易。交易员按利润分成，并可领取少量预支。有交易经验会被考虑，但不是必要条件。

在那个年代，赚钱的交易系统被视为命根子，没人会登报教人。更反常的是“经验不是必要条件”——这就像一支职业球队公开招人，不问身高体重，也不问会不会打球。

广告预算只有一万五千美元，藏在报纸后页。丹尼斯后来说，他并不是为了招几个便宜的交易员。他要解决一个和搭档争了十年的问题：交易能力究竟是天赋，还是可以教出来的？

二、争论的双方#

丹尼斯属于“培养派”。他出身芝加哥南区，十几岁就在交易池里做跑单员，没有名校学历，也没有正式训练。到 1983 年，他三十七岁，已经用几百美元的起点赚到了数以亿计的财富，人送外号“交易池王子”。他的结论很自然：如果我能学会，别人也能。

在他眼里，市场像一盘大富翁游戏：价格、赔率、规则、仓位，都是客观的、可以拆解的。他甚至不太把钱当钱，而当成记分牌——“赚到五千，就能下更大的注；赚到两万五，再下更大的注。”这种对杠杆的理解，他认为也可以教。

埃克哈特（William Eckhardt）属于“天赋派”。他是丹尼斯的高中同学，芝加哥大学数学硕士，做过四年数理逻辑博士研究，是两人中真正的数学家。他相信市场里存在某种无法写进规则的东西：直觉、盘感、天生的风险嗅觉。他不认为把丹尼斯的“心理软件”装进别人脑子里就能复制成功。

但埃克哈特并非看不起普通人。相反，他见过太多带着巨额遗产进场、最后输光的富家子弟。他说过一句很锋利的话：“带着几千美元、感觉自己输不起的人，比带着几百万进场的人更值得下注。” 因为前者能感受到痛，后者感受不到。

三、为什么这是一场“真实验”#

两人没有停在嘴上。丹尼斯说：那就招人、训练、给钱，看结果。埃克哈特同意。这是一场事先设计好的智力实验，而不是事后编出来的传奇。

实验有几个关键设计：

  • 公开招募：不看学历、不看履历、不看行业背景，只看申请材料、测试和面试。
  • 统一训练：两期学员，分别在 1983 年底和 1984 年接受培训，学同一套方法。
  • 真实资金：学员用丹尼斯的钱交易，拿利润的 15%，亏损由丹尼斯承担。
  • 可比较的结果：所有人面对同一套规则、同一段市场，业绩可以横向比较。

“海龟”这个名字来自丹尼斯的一次新加坡旅行。他参观了一个养龟场，看到一池子密密麻麻的小龟，说：“我们就像新加坡养海龟那样，养交易员。”

两期加起来，走进教室的有二十多人，但其中只有大约一半是真正通过广告选拔、只替丹尼斯交易的“海龟”。另外一些人是公司同事、朋友，或者被允许旁听的人。这也解释了后来关于“到底谁是海龟”的长期争议。

四、实验真正回答了什么#

先给结论：在“能否学会一套机械规则”这个层面上，丹尼斯赢了。 埃克哈特多年后公开承认，他原本认为交易员身上有某种无法机械化的东西，“我被证明错了。总的来说，他们学得非常好。交易能否被教会，答案只能是一个毫无保留的‘是’。”

但要注意，这场实验并不是严格意义上的随机对照试验。申请者经过筛选：数学能力、考试分数、对游戏和概率的兴趣、能否放下自我，都是标准。也就是说，它不是从全人类里随机抽人，而是从“愿意应征且看起来可教”的人里抽人。

所以它证明的是一件更窄、但依然重要的事：

一群没有专业背景的普通人，在给定规则、资金和纪律约束后，可以学会按一套趋势跟踪方法稳定执行，并取得可观收益。

它没有证明“任何人都能成为丹尼斯”，也没有证明“学会规则就必然赚钱”。第 5 课和第 14 课会看到：实验结束后，同一批人走向了完全不同的结局。

五、为什么这个故事今天仍值得学#

因为这套方法没有过时。海龟规则的核心——顺势、分散、按波动率定仓位、控制回撤——至今仍被许多管理数十亿美元的期货基金使用。它不依赖内幕、预测或宏观判断，只依赖价格和风险管理。

对今天的我们，最重要的不是“1983 年有人赚了大钱”，而是：如果一套方法能被写清楚、被检验、被重复执行，那么一个愿意下笨功夫的人，就有机会学会它。 这正是这门课存在的前提。

本课要点#

  • 海龟实验源于丹尼斯与埃克哈特关于“交易能否被教会”的十年争论。
  • 丹尼斯相信培养，埃克哈特相信天赋；两人用公开招募、统一训练和真实资金来裁决。
  • 广告强调“无需经验”，实际筛选却看重数学能力、概率思维、游戏兴趣和放下自我的能力。
  • 实验证明的是“普通人可以学会执行一套机械规则”，而不是“人人都能成为交易大师”。
  • “海龟”之名来自新加坡养龟场，与策略本身无关。

自测#

  1. 丹尼斯和埃克哈特的核心分歧是什么？各自最有力的论据是什么？
  2. 为什么说这场实验不是严格的随机对照试验？这会影响它的说服力吗？
  3. 如果有人告诉你“海龟实验证明任何人都能靠交易致富”，这句话哪里不准确？
参考答案

1. 分歧是交易能力能否被拆成规则并教会。丹尼斯认为自己的成功来自可学习的方法，且他从几百美元起家、没有正式训练；埃克哈特认为存在无法机械化的直觉，且真正的数学家才是系统背后的关键。

2. 学员经过测试和面试筛选，并非随机抽样；训练内容、资金分配、市场环境也由丹尼斯控制。它仍是强有力的自然实验，但不能推出“对所有人有效”。

3. 实验证明的是“可以学会按规则交易并获得正期望”，不是“必然致富”。收益还取决于资金规模、市场周期、执行纪律和风险控制；实验结束后学员的分化恰好说明这一点。

What you will learn: what the Turtle experiment was really testing, where each man stood, and what the result can and cannot prove.

1. A small ad in 1983#

In the autumn of 1983, Richard Dennis — one of Chicago's most profitable futures traders — ran a classified ad in the Wall Street Journal, Barron's and the International Herald Tribune. It was short and strange:

Commodity futures trader wanted. A small group of applicants will be trained in proprietary trading concepts. Successful candidates will trade solely for the firm and may not trade for themselves or others. Traders receive a percentage of profits and a small draw. Prior trading experience will be considered, but is not necessary.

In that era, a profitable trading system was treated as a family jewel. Nobody advertised lessons. Even stranger was "experience not necessary" — as if a professional sports team advertised open tryouts without asking whether you could play.

The advertising budget was only $15,000, buried in the back pages. Dennis was not shopping for cheap employees. He was trying to settle an argument that had run for a decade with his partner: is trading a gift, or a skill that can be taught?

2. The two sides#

Dennis was the nurture camp. He grew up on Chicago's South Side, started as a runner in the trading pits as a teenager, and had no elite degree or formal training. By 1983, at thirty-seven, he had turned a few hundred dollars into hundreds of millions and was nicknamed the "Prince of the Pit." His conclusion was natural: if I could learn it, so can someone else.

To him the market resembled a board game. Prices, odds, rules and position size were objective and could be taken apart. He barely treated money as money; it was a scoreboard. "If I make $5,000, I can bet more and maybe make $25,000; then bet that and get to $250,000." That understanding of leverage, he believed, was teachable too.

William Eckhardt was the nature camp. Dennis's high-school friend, he held a master's in mathematics from the University of Chicago and had spent four years in doctoral research in mathematical logic. He was the real mathematician of the pair. He believed something in trading could not be written into rules: intuition, a feel for the tape, an inborn sense of risk. You could not simply install Dennis's mental software in another brain.

Yet Eckhardt did not look down on ordinary people. He had watched too many heirs enter markets with fortunes and lose them. His line was sharp: "You're much better off going into the market on a shoestring, feeling that you can't afford to lose. I'd rather bet on somebody starting out with a few thousand dollars than on somebody who came in with millions." The first feels pain; the second does not.

3. Why it was a real experiment#

They did not leave it as talk. Dennis said: hire people, train them, fund them, and see. Eckhardt agreed. This was an experiment designed in advance, not a legend assembled afterwards.

Its key features:

  • Open recruitment. No filter on credentials or industry background — only applications, tests and interviews.
  • Common training. Two classes, starting in late 1983 and 1984, learned the same method.
  • Real money. The students traded Dennis's capital, kept 15% of profits, and did not bear the losses.
  • Comparable results. Everyone faced the same rules and the same markets, so performance could be compared.

The name "Turtles" came from a trip to Singapore, where Dennis visited a turtle-breeding farm. Looking at a vat of squirming hatchlings, he said something like: "We are going to grow traders just like they grow turtles in Singapore."

Across the two classes, more than two dozen people sat in the training room, but only about half were official Turtles — selected through the ad and trading only Dennis's money. Others were colleagues, friends, or people allowed to sit in. That is one reason the question "who counts as a Turtle?" has never been settled.

4. What the experiment actually proved#

The short answer: on the narrow question of whether a mechanical rule set can be taught, Dennis won. Eckhardt later conceded publicly that he had assumed a trader added something that could not be captured in a mechanical program. "I was proven wrong. By and large, they learned to trade exceedingly well. The answer to whether trading can be taught has to be an unqualified yes."

But this was not a clean randomized controlled trial. Applicants were screened for mathematical aptitude, test scores, interest in games and probability, and the ability to subordinate ego. The sample was drawn from people who answered the ad and looked teachable, not from humanity at random.

So the result is narrower than "anyone can do it," but still important:

A group of ordinary people without professional backgrounds, given rules, capital and discipline, can learn to execute a trend-following method consistently and make substantial profits.

It did not prove that everyone can become Dennis, and it did not prove that knowing the rules guarantees money. Lessons 5 and 14 show that after the program, the same group diverged dramatically.

5. Why the story still matters#

Because the method has not expired. The core of Turtle trading — follow trends, diversify, size by volatility, control drawdown — is still used by futures funds managing billions. It relies on price and risk management, not inside information, forecasts or macro opinion.

For us, the important sentence is not "some people got rich in 1983." It is: if a method can be written down, tested and repeated, a person willing to do unglamorous work has a chance to learn it. That premise is why this course exists.

Key points#

  • The experiment grew out of a decade-long argument between Dennis (nurture) and Eckhardt (nature).
  • Dennis believed his skill was learnable; Eckhardt believed something ineffable separated good traders from the rest.
  • The ad said "no experience necessary," but selection still favoured math ability, probabilistic thinking, games and a teachable ego.
  • The experiment supports "ordinary people can learn to execute a mechanical system," not "anyone can get rich trading."
  • "Turtles" came from a Singapore turtle farm, not from the strategy.

Self-test#

  1. What exactly did Dennis and Eckhardt disagree about, and what was each man's strongest argument?
  2. Why was this not a strict randomized experiment? Does that weaken the conclusion?
  3. Someone says: "The Turtles prove anyone can get rich trading." What is wrong with that sentence?
Answers

1. Whether trading skill can be reduced to teachable rules. Dennis pointed to his own rise from a few hundred dollars with no formal training; Eckhardt pointed to the ineffable element he believed separated great traders, and to the mathematical work behind the systems.

2. Students were screened and interviewed, not randomly sampled; Dennis controlled training, funding and the market period. It remains a powerful natural experiment, but it cannot establish "works for everyone."

3. It confuses "can learn to trade a positive-expectancy system" with "will necessarily become rich." Results also depend on capital, market cycles, discipline and risk control — and the Turtles' later divergence shows exactly that.
