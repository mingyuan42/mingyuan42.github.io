Part Two · Lesson 06

Expectation

About 5 min read·Based on Michael W. Covel's The Complete TurtleTrader

What you will learn: how to judge whether a method can make money over the long run with one formula — and why "90% accuracy" can be a trap.

1. Ask the right question#

Most beginners ask: "How accurate is this method?"

The Turtles were trained to ask something else:

Over the long run, how much does each trade earn me on average?

In casino language, this is the edge. A casino does not care who wins the next hand; it cares how many dollars it keeps per hundred wagered over time. Dennis wanted his trading to resemble being the house.

2. One formula#

Expectation (E) is simple:

E = (PW × AW) − (PL × AL)

Where:

  • PW = winning percentage
  • AW = average winner
  • PL = losing percentage (= 1 − PW)
  • AL = average loser (as a positive number)

Example one#

A system wins 50% of the time, average winner $500, average loser $350:

E = (0.50 × 500) − (0.50 × 350)
  = 250 − 175
  = $75 per trade

Over time, each trade is worth $75 on average.

Example two#

Another system wins only 40% of the time, but its average winner is $1,000 and its average loser is still $350:

E = (0.40 × 1,000) − (0.60 × 350)
  = 400 − 210
  = $190 per trade

The second system has 2.5 times the edge of the first, despite a win rate ten points lower.

Hence the Turtles' refrain: percent accuracy means nothing.

3. Break-even win rate#

Given average winner and average loser, the minimum win rate needed to break even is:

Break-even PW = AL ÷ (AW + AL)
  • System one: 350 ÷ (500 + 350) = 41.2%. Anything above that is profitable long run.
  • System two: 350 ÷ (1,000 + 350) = 25.9%. Anything above that is profitable.

Now turn it around. A system advertising "90% accuracy" that wins $10 but loses $200 has:

0.9 × 10 − 0.1 × 200 = 9 − 20 = −$11 per trade

Nine wins, one loss, still losing. High accuracy feels good, and it is exactly how small gains accumulate until one large loss wipes them out.

4. The baseball analogy#

Dennis used baseball:

The average batter hits maybe .280, and the average system might be successful 35 percent of the time.

What matters is not how often you hit but whether the hits are singles or home runs. A .280 hitter who hits home runs is worth far more than a .320 hitter who only singles.

Trend-following systems are built this way: most trades are small losses, a few are large gains. You might make ten trades, lose small on seven and win big on three, and end up positive. Therefore:

  • Losing streaks are the system breathing, not the system breaking.
  • Judge a system over hundreds of trades, not the last five.
  • If you demand that every trade wins, you structurally exclude every trend system.

5. Run the numbers on real data#

The book reports monthly figures for seven Turtle-style traders from inception to August 2006. Take Liz Cheval:

  • Average winning month: +12.45%
  • Average losing month: −6.64%
  • Winning months: 49.62%
E = (0.4962 × 12.45) − (0.5038 × 6.64)
  = 6.18 − 3.35
  ≈ 2.83% per month

She wins less than half her months (49.62%) and still earns about +2.83% per month in expectation. Compare the indexes:

Market Avg winning month Avg losing month Winning months Expectation
Dow Jones 3.87% −3.85% 58.09% 0.63%
NASDAQ 4.98% −4.61% 57.75% 0.93%
S&P 500 3.83% −3.92% 58.37% 0.60%

The indexes win more often — nearly 58% of months — and earn far less. The reason is single: the Turtles' winning months are much larger than their losing months.

That is the whole secret of trend following: do not chase frequency, chase magnitude.

6. Expectation and risk management are different jobs#

Expectation tells you whether a method has an edge. It does not tell you whether you will survive long enough to collect it.

A positive-expectation system can still ruin you if each bet is too large — that is the next lesson. Eckhardt's summary is exact:

The important thing is to limit portfolio risk. The trades will take care of themselves.

Key points#

  • E = (PW × AW) − (PL × AL) measures average profit per trade over the long run.
  • Low accuracy is not necessarily bad: 40% at 1,000:350 beats 50% at 500:350 by 2.5 times.
  • Break-even PW = AL ÷ (AW + AL).
  • High accuracy with small wins and large losses equals negative expectation — the most common way retail traders die.
  • Trend systems normally lose small often and win big rarely; streaks do not invalidate the system.
  • Expectation answers "can it make money"; risk management answers "will you live to collect it."

Self-test#

  1. A system wins 35% of the time, average winner 1,200, average loser 400. What is its expectation and break-even win rate?
  2. "My method only loses twice a month — 90% accuracy." Which two numbers do you still need before judging it?
  3. Why is judging a system on the last ten trades statistically useless?
Answers

1. E = 0.35 × 1,200 − 0.65 × 400 = 420 − 260 = 160 per trade. Break-even PW = 400 ÷ 1,600 = 25%. At 35%, the edge is clearly positive.

2. Average winner and average loser. If it wins 50 and loses 800, E = 0.9 × 50 − 0.1 × 800 = 45 − 80 = −35 per trade — a guaranteed long-run loss.

3. Trend-system returns are concentrated in a few large trades. A ten-trade sample may contain none of them; the variance is enormous and the sample cannot represent the long-run distribution.
