Part Four · Lesson 14

After the Turtles

About 9 min read·Based on Michael W. Covel's The Complete TurtleTrader

What you will learn: why one set of rules produced wildly different lives, the gap between a successful experiment and a successful career, and what Dennis's second act teaches about drawdowns and trust.

1. After the fax#

After the fax in early 1988, the Turtles scattered.

Some became Wall Street regulars:

  • Jerry Parker, the small-town Virginia accountant, founded his own firm and averaged better than 20% a year from 1988 through 2006 — from a quiet office outside Richmond, across from a hair and nail salon.
  • Liz Cheval founded EMC Capital and was one of the few Turtles to manage client money publicly for decades.
  • Paul Rabar brought a pianist's discipline to trading and built his own research firm.
  • Tom Shanks, Howard Seidler, Jim DiMaria and others ran money or businesses of their own.
  • Bill Eckhardt, who wrote much of the actual mathematics, had a long, successful career; his hedge fund later approached $800 million.

Others drifted out of the industry, or never repeated the early results.

2. Same rules, different endings#

This is one of the most important questions in the book.

Everyone learned the same rules, used the same sizing arithmetic, sat in the same classroom for two weeks. Why did some become very rich while others went back to a job?

Survivorship bias#

Start with a statistical fact: the "successful Turtles" we read about are a filtered sample. At the end of the program, only some kept trading and survived; those who blew up, quit or changed careers rarely make the story.

So you cannot conclude "learn the Turtle rules and you will succeed." The rules are necessary, not sufficient.

Mental toughness#

Larry Hite, another hedge-fund pioneer, was blunt:

The people who are very good and long-lasting are tough. If life goes against them, they stick to their game. There is a certain amount of mental toughness to have clarity. Toughness is just the ability to roll with the punches. They don't get disappointed by losses. Some people when they lose, they don't get back up. Think about the Turtles who failed at trading. What did the failures have in common? Maybe the ones who failed just gave up. They were not tough.

Hite also pointed at Dennis's 1986 memo cutting size by half as an act of toughness: admitting an error, ignoring the ego, and fixing it.

But Dennis never screened for toughness or entrepreneurial drive. Inside the greenhouse — where he supplied the capital and absorbed the losses — everyone looked tough. Only when the greenhouse came down did the difference show.

Entrepreneurial drive#

Salem Abraham summarised the winners:

I think they have a self-confidence or charisma to run a business. There's a drive to go out and do it. Then there is a drive to want to do it. Some people will just say, "Oh, hey, I made some money. That's all I need."

Rules keep you from losing big; only drive turns a system into a business.

3. Dennis's second act#

Dennis did not disappear.

He returned in 1994. Through September 1998 his compound annual return was about +63%; 1995 was +108.9% and 1996 +112.7% — two straight triple-digit years.

In August 1998, with Russia defaulting and Long-Term Capital Management imploding, Wall Street was in agony. Dennis made +13.5% that month and was up about +45% year to date. Trend followers profited while the fund run by two Nobel laureates lost billions.

He was still the same high-risk, high-reward trader — his ticket to the Hall of Fame and his Achilles' heel in one.

Then, on 29 September 2000, Dennis Trading Group stopped trading and liquidated client accounts.

One investor explained:

Dennis Trading Group was −50% down in June but then made a slight recovery in July. But we finally broke through the −50% mark to −52%. You still can trade and try to recover when you're down 50%, but you run the risk of falling to −60% or −70%, and there's no turning back from there.

The bitter part: clients redeemed in autumn 2000, which turned out to be a bottom for trend following. Over the next twelve months, many of his peers gained 100% or more.

Clients panicked at the low and paid the highest price.

4. Drawdowns and trust#

Dennis defended the 1988 episode: clients did not understand his style and lost faith at the first drawdown; Drexel's marketers sold only the good times.

Half of that is fair. Trend following produces deep drawdowns by design; if the people funding you were never educated, they will run at the worst moment.

But the manager owns part of it too: if you never told people how much they could lose and for how long, you have no right to demand faith in the abyss.

This is the most practical lesson in the whole story, whether you manage other people's money, your family's money, or your own:

  • Define the drawdown expectation before discussing returns.
  • Use rules, not mood, to decide when to cut size or stop.
  • Decisions made at the bottom are usually wrong — so write them down in advance.

5. Second-generation Turtles: you do not need the lottery#

The real point of the book is not the twenty people who answered an ad. It is that people who were not chosen can learn this too.

Salem Abraham lived in Canadian, Texas, in a family of ranchers and businessmen. A cousin of his was married to Jerry Parker. In a casual conversation Parker explained the rules: given the state of the market, you put on certain trades.

Abraham did not believe it at first, but remembered. He called Parker, visited, and built the system himself. No Dennis capital, no two-week class, no greenhouse — his own money and his own risk from day one.

The results:

  • Twice clients left after weak performance; twice he regrouped and made new highs.
  • He invested $1.5 million in water rights sold to Amarillo and got $9 million out.
  • He put $1.5 million into the Chicago Mercantile Exchange IPO and got about $13 million out.
  • He made his first million before turning twenty-five.

His hiring mimics Dennis almost exactly: no Ivy League degrees; most employees came from local feedlots or gas-drilling companies. He hired a clerk from a feedlot who said, "This beats shovelling manure at 6 a.m."

Abraham matters because he proves you did not need to see the 1983 ad to walk this path. You only need to know the philosophy exists, then build it yourself.

6. Two halves of the story#

The author splits the Turtle story in two:

  • Part one: the experiment. On a relatively level playing field designed by Dennis, the rules were shown to work. This proves nurture beats nature.
  • Part two: the aftermath. Each person faces the real world alone and human nature returns. This proves rules alone are not enough — you also need toughness, drive and a little obsessiveness.

It is like a professional sports draft: every year college stars are picked with fanfare, and every year at least one "can't miss" prospect misses. Talent alone is never enough.

Cultivating that extra drive requires deliberate practice:

  • Charlie Munger: "In my whole life, I have known no wise people over a broad subject matter area who didn't read all the time — none, zero."
  • Eddie Lampert learned Buffett by reverse-engineering his thinking: "Putting myself in his shoes at that time, could I understand why he made the investments?"
  • Second-generation Turtles did the same with Dennis.

7. The market does not care about you#

Finally, remember: the market does not care about you personally. Not your gender, culture, religion, race — nor how much you lost yesterday.

It is one of the last places with almost no barrier to entry: anyone can put up cash and take a swing at making a lot of money.

Which also means nobody is coming to save you. Rules, arithmetic, discipline and toughness have to be grown from the inside.

In the autumn of 2006, at the baggage claim at Chicago O'Hare, futures legend Richard Sandor smiled and said to the author:

You do know that Richard Dennis is trading again?

That sentence is the book's real ending: the game is not over. As long as you are willing to bet by the rules, you are still in it.

Key points#

  • After the program, Parker, Cheval, Rabar, Shanks, Seidler and DiMaria thrived; others left.
  • Same rules, different lives: survivorship bias, mental toughness and entrepreneurial drive.
  • Hite: the long-lasting are tough; the failures often simply gave up.
  • Dennis returned in 1994, posted triple-digit years in 1995–96 and a huge August 1998, then liquidated at −52% in September 2000.
  • Clients redeemed at the bottom; peers then gained 100%+ — the most expensive decisions are made at lows.
  • Second-generation Turtle Salem Abraham proves you need not be chosen to learn this and build a business.
  • Two halves: the experiment proves training works; the aftermath demands toughness and drive.
  • The market does not care about you, but it is open to everyone.

Self-test#

  1. Use survivorship bias to explain why successful Turtles do not prove "learn the rules and you will succeed."
  2. What does Dennis's 2000 liquidation teach about communicating drawdowns to whoever funds you?
  3. Why is Salem Abraham's story more important than the original Turtles' story?
Answers

1. We only observe those who survived and kept trading; failures leave the sample. Rules are necessary but unobserved variables — toughness, capital, drive — also determine outcomes, so survivor success cannot be attributed to rules alone.

2. State the possible drawdown and its likely duration before money is committed, and pre-agree the rules for cutting size or stopping. Otherwise funders panic at the deepest point and managers break their own systems under pressure. Communicate first; do not explain afterwards.

3. The original Turtles were a small, selected sample with a lottery element. Abraham was not chosen, had none of Dennis's capital or greenhouse, and reproduced both the method and a business — proving the philosophy is learnable and executable by ordinary people.
