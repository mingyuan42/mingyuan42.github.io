Part Three · Lesson 11

Entries — Follow Price Only

About 7 min read·Based on Michael W. Covel's The Complete TurtleTrader

What you will learn: Systems One and Two, the S1 filter, the fail-safe role of S2, and why the exact parameters are not holy writ.

1. Breakouts: no calling tops or bottoms#

The Turtle entry rule is one thing: price makes a recent new high or new low.

  • New 20-day high → buy.
  • New 20-day low → sell short.

No valuation, no news, no "it feels like it has fallen enough." In Eckhardt's words:

Pure price systems are close enough to the North Pole that any departure tends to bring you farther south.

Meaning: price alone is as close as you get to market truth; every subjective variable you add moves you further away.

It also means the Turtles were the opposite of "buy low, sell high": they bought what was getting more expensive and sold what was getting cheaper. They had no long or short bias.

2. S1: four weeks in, two weeks out#

System One (S1) was the workhorse:

  • Entry: a new 20-trading-day (four-week) high → long; a new 20-day low → short.
  • Exit: after a long, a new 10-trading-day (two-week) low closes it; after a short, a new 10-day high.

Note the exit is an opposite breakout, not a fixed profit target. As long as the trend keeps making new highs you hold; you only concede "this trend is over" when the market itself prints a two-week opposite extreme.

That is "let profits run" in mechanical form: you do not decide how far it runs; the market does.

3. The S1 filter: skip after a winner#

S1 carried an odd-looking filter:

If the last 20-day breakout signal was a winner, skip the next 20-day breakout signal.

The conditions are strict:

  • Even if you did not actually take the previous signal, it counts.
  • Even if it was only theoretically a winner, it counts.
  • Whether the previous signal was long or short is irrelevant.
  • Only if the previous breakout ended in a 2N loss may you take the current one.

Why design it that way?

Because in choppy markets, two consecutive same-direction breakouts are often false breakout followed by false breakout. After the first breakout works, the move is often largely spent, and the second entry tends to buy a short-term high. The filter's purpose is: in environments prone to repeated failure, take one fewer shot.

It trades "might miss" for "fewer wasted trades."

4. S2: the fail-safe for filtered-out trends#

The filter has an obvious hole: what if the skipped breakout was the start of a huge trend? You stand aside while the market leaves without you.

The Turtles' answer was System Two (S2), the fail-safe:

  • Entry: a 55-trading-day (eleven-week) breakout.
  • Exit: a 20-trading-day (four-week) opposite breakout.

S2 is slower and rarer, but when it fires it usually marks a larger trend. What S1 filters out, S2 picks up.

The book's yen example is typical: on 16 February 1995 the September yen made a four-week high, so S1 entered the next trading day; the position was held through every subsequent new high and exited on a new two-week low on 26 April.

5. Half and half#

S1 and S2 were not alternatives. The Turtles typically allocated half their money to each.

Some blended them:

  • Mike Carr combined S1 and S2 to get more entry and exit points and smooth his results.
  • Jeff Gordon preferred S1 but mixed in S2 for a smoother curve.

The principle: the two systems cover different time scales; together they are steadier than either alone. The short-term system reacts fast and produces more false signals; the long-term system reacts slowly, trades rarely, but catches larger moves.

6. S3: the "do whatever you want" account#

There was a third system, which Dennis called the flair account (S3).

The rules were almost absent: do anything you like, but do not lose more than $50,000. One dollar past that and you are out of the program.

It was a deliberate experiment: give the Turtles a small account of complete freedom and see whether freedom would destroy them.

The result: within about six weeks almost everyone voluntarily shut it down. Erle Keefer explained:

They had already lived emotionally losing seven out of ten trades. They knew that was the right thing to do. That was the only way you were ever going to hook the real trend.

S3's value was not profit; it was proof that when the rules are removed, people return to rules — because they had seen why the rules work.

7. Robustness: 20 and 55 are not the Holy Grail#

Over the years, traders who learned the Turtle rules became obsessed with the exact numbers, arguing whether a 50-day breakout beats a 51-day breakout.

That is missing the forest for the tree.

The principle that matters is robustness:

A minor change of a variable in any robust trading system should not cause significant performance changes. If it does, you are in trouble.

Jerry Parker makes the point with the Mount Lucas Management Index, a trend-following index based on a 52-week moving average running since the 1960s. Parker says the core simple moving-average/breakout system drives about two-thirds of profits; if the little extras — early entries, quick exits, volatility filters — became the main source of returns, "we're going to be in bad shape."

His conclusion: parameters can be lengthened, but the moment it needs too much thinking, too much analysis, too much fancy work, "it is going to be a very bad situation."

Trader Ed Seykota called this over-complication "math-turbation."

8. How you might use it#

Three practical takeaways:

  1. Pick a breakout length and apply it consistently. 20/10 or 55/20 are both reasonable starting points; do not switch mid-stream.
  2. If you add a filter, know what it defends against. The Turtle filter defends against repeated false breakouts in chop, not "raising accuracy."
  3. Test on paper before risking money. You need to see the losing streaks and drawdowns yourself in order to hold the system when it hurts.

Key points#

  • Entries depend only on price breakouts — no forecasting, no fundamentals.
  • S1: 20-day breakout entry, 10-day opposite breakout exit.
  • S1 filter: skip after a winning breakout (even untaken, even theoretical); direction irrelevant; a prior 2N loss allows entry.
  • S2: 55-day entry, 20-day opposite exit, catching what S1 filters out.
  • Capital is typically split half to S1, half to S2.
  • S3 was a "lose no more than $50,000" freedom account that most Turtles shut within weeks.
  • Robustness matters more than exact parameters.

Self-test#

  1. A market makes a 20-day high and the Turtles go long. It keeps making new highs. When do they exit?
  2. The last 20-day breakout was not traded but would theoretically have won. A new 20-day breakout appears. Do you take it? If the move continues, do you still get a chance?
  3. Why is "changing 50 days to 51 days destroys performance" a danger sign?
Answers

1. Only when price makes a new 10-day low (a two-week opposite breakout). New highs in between are not exit reasons.

2. No — the filter counts theoretical winners regardless of whether you traded. If the move continues, S2's 55-day breakout gives a second entry.

3. It implies the returns depend on a very narrow parameter range, likely overfitting to history, and will break when conditions shift. A robust system performs similarly under small parameter changes.
