Part One · Lesson 03

One Ad, a Thousand Résumés

About 6 min read·Based on Michael W. Covel's The Complete TurtleTrader

What you will learn: how the Turtles were selected, and what Dennis and Eckhardt were really screening for — and why intelligence alone was not the criterion.

1. What happened after the ad#

Responses arrived from everywhere — more than a thousand in total. They were wildly varied: one applicant wrote his case on a coconut; another offered a single sentence, "I think I can make money for you"; others sent long explanations of why the world had overlooked them.

Those who survived the first cut received a formal letter and a test. The letter was blunt: successful candidates would complete a short training period and a trial trading period, then receive 15% of profits. Everyone had to relocate to Chicago.

The test had two parts.

The first was 63 true/false questions, answered quickly. They looked easy and were not. They tested default trading beliefs, not book knowledge. A sample of the ideas:

  • "Trade long or short, but not both."
  • "Trade the same number of contracts in all markets."
  • "If you have $100,000 to risk, you should risk $25,000 on every trade."
  • "When you enter, you should know where to exit if a loss occurs."
  • "You can never go broke taking profits."
  • "The majority of traders are always wrong."
  • "Average profits should be about three or four times average losses."
  • "A very high percentage of trades should be profitable."

Some statements are true, some false, but all touch position sizing, stops, payoff and win rate. A beginner can answer some by common sense; someone infected by bad habits reveals himself fast.

The second part was five one-sentence essays: a book or film you like and why; a historical figure you like and why; why you want to succeed at this job; a risky thing you have done and why; anything else you want to add. There were further questions about personal strengths and weaknesses, and whether the applicant would rather be good or lucky.

2. The interview: looking for someone teachable#

Survivors were invited to Chicago, where Dennis and Eckhardt interviewed everyone personally. The atmosphere was often surprisingly casual; the questions were not.

A classic: "What if you get stopped out of the same long trade five times in a row?" Paul Rabar answered, "If it goes up again, I will buy it again." That was the answer Dennis wanted — a trader not frightened by the last loss.

Another applicant, Erle Keefer, fell into a discussion of British empiricism and Berkeley's Hylas and Philonous with Dennis, then faced Eckhardt on the central limit theorem. Keefer later concluded that one thing Dennis was testing was the ability to suspend your belief in what you take to be reality.

Taken together, they were not looking for "the person most likely to get rich." They were looking for:

  • Mathematical and probabilistic aptitude — no degree required, but a natural feel for odds.
  • Interest in games and systems — many Turtles played chess or backgammon and mentioned it on their résumés.
  • The ability to treat money abstractly — as calculable chips, not as personal worth.
  • A teachable ego — willingness to be a blank slate, to act first and understand later.
  • Emotional stability — the capacity to function through losing streaks and uncertainty.

Michael Shannon prepared hard. He went to the basement of the Chicago Tribune and read everything he could find about Dennis; more than 90% of the test answers, he later discovered, were in those articles. He also learned that Dennis disliked shoes and suits, so he wore an old sport coat, jeans and topsiders with no socks. They discovered a shared boyhood game of Risk, and the ice broke.

3. Who actually got in#

The people who entered the classroom were an odd mix:

  • Jerry Parker — a small-town accountant from Lynchburg, Virginia, and a devout evangelical Christian, who became the most successful Turtle.
  • Paul Rabar — a classically trained pianist who dropped out of UCLA medical school, and the most experienced trader among the hires.
  • Mike Cavallo — a chess master who could play five opponents blindfolded.
  • Mike Carr — a well-known Dungeons & Dragons writer who initially could not read a futures chart.
  • Liz Cheval — later founder of EMC Capital and one of the few Turtles to run client money publicly for decades.
  • Erle Keefer — the oldest hire at thirty-seven, a US Air Force Academy graduate who flew rescue helicopters in Vietnam and was a founding member of LIFFE in London.
  • Curtis Faith — young, fiercely competitive, at one point controlling a large share of the program's capital, and later its most controversial figure.

In one room: a Harvard MBA and a recent high-school graduate; a devout believer and an Eastern European card counter; artists, chess players, musicians and accountants. Their common trait was that they passed the same screen and were willing to follow the same rules.

4. The contract and the two classes#

Successful candidates signed a demanding agreement: for roughly five years they could trade only for Dennis, not for themselves or anyone else; they could not disclose the proprietary methods, with confidentiality surviving the program; they received 15% of profits plus a small draw, and did not bear losses personally.

The first class began at the end of 1983, with two weeks of formal training starting January 1984. The second class began in 1984, with training shortened to one week. Classes were held at the Union League Club in downtown Chicago, an old-fashioned gentlemen's club where jackets and ties were mandatory — including for Dennis.

Because "Turtle" was never precisely defined, the headcount has been disputed ever since: some say thirteen, others more than twenty. What is certain is that roughly twenty core members were selected through the ad and traded only Dennis's money; the rest were colleagues, friends or observers.

Key points#

  • Selection looked for probabilistic thinking, a teachable ego and emotional stability — not genius.
  • The 63 true/false questions probed default beliefs about sizing, stops, payoff and accuracy.
  • Interviews tested reactions to repeated losses and attitudes toward uncertainty.
  • The group was extremely diverse, which supports the claim that the method is not tied to a particular background.
  • The contract was strict: five years exclusive, confidentiality, 15% of profits, losses borne by Dennis.
  • The exact number of Turtles is disputed; about twenty were core members.

Self-test#

  1. Why use 63 "simple" true/false questions instead of a test of trading knowledge?
  2. Why was Rabar's answer — "If it goes up again, I will buy it again" — a winning answer?
  3. Does the selection process support the claim that "any random person could have succeeded"? Why or why not?
Answers

1. The questions exposed intuitive beliefs about risk, sizing and accuracy. Knowledge can be memorised; default instincts are harder to fake under time pressure.

2. It showed he could separate the outcome of the last trade from the validity of the signal, which is exactly the psychology required to execute a mechanical system.

3. No. The pool was diverse but clearly screened for maths, odds-thinking, game experience and emotional stability. The experiment shows that people meeting those conditions can learn, not that conditions are irrelevant.
