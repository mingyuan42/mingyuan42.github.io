Part Three · Lesson 09

N — The Market's Breath

About 6 min read·Based on Michael W. Covel's The Complete TurtleTrader

What you will learn: how to compute N from true range, and why N is the common ruler for position size, stops and pyramiding.

1. Why you need a ruler#

Suppose two people each buy one corn contract:

  • A buys in a quiet year, when corn moves 3 cents a day.
  • B buys in a wild year, when corn moves 15 cents a day.

Same "one contract," five times the risk. If you manage risk by counting contracts, you have no idea what you are actually risking.

The Turtles needed a ruler that was comparable across markets and across time. That ruler is N, also called ATR (Average True Range).

In one sentence: N is how far this market typically travels in a day.

2. True range: how far a day actually went#

First compute the true range (TR) for a day. Take the maximum of three distances:

  1. Today's high minus today's low
  2. The distance between yesterday's close and today's high (absolute value)
  3. The distance between yesterday's close and today's low (absolute value)

Why look at yesterday's close? Because markets gap. If yesterday closed at 500 and today opens at 520 and trades up to 524, then "today's high minus today's low" might be only 4 — but measured from yesterday's close, the market has travelled 24. Gaps are volatility too; do not miss them.

Take the maximum, make it absolute, and you have the day's TR.

3. N is a 20-day average#

A single day's TR is noisy, so the Turtles averaged the last 20 trading days of TR. That is N.

Using the book's Kansas City wheat example (September 2006):

Date High Low Close TR1 (H−L) TR2 (|prev C−H|) TR3 (|prev C−L|) TR
07/03 521.50 511.25 516.50 — — — —
07/05 524.00 513.00 521.50 11.00 7.50 3.50 11.00
07/06 523.50 515.50 518.00 8.00 2.00 6.00 8.00
07/07 515.00 505.50 506.00 9.50 3.00 12.50 12.50
07/11 527.50 515.00 524.00 12.50 16.50 4.00 16.50
07/13 514.00 492.00 493.00 22.00 4.50 26.50 26.50

Take 07/13:

  • TR1 = 514.00 − 492.00 = 22.00
  • TR2 = |previous close 518.50 − 514.00| = 4.50
  • TR3 = |518.50 − 492.00| = 26.50
  • TR = the maximum = 26.50

Add the 20 daily TRs:

11.00 + 8.00 + 12.50 + 7.00 + 16.50 + 12.00 + 26.50 + 9.00 + 14.50 + 7.50
+ 13.00 + 19.00 + 14.50 + 7.50 + 19.00 + 8.50 + 7.00 + 5.00 + 10.00 + 10.75
= 238.75
N = 238.75 ÷ 20 = 11.9375 ≈ 11.94

Meaning: over this stretch, Kansas City wheat typically moved about 11.94 cents a day.

(The book's sidebar for your own account suggests a 15-day average is also fine; shorter is more responsive, longer is smoother. The classroom standard was 20 days.)

4. N is a price and also a dollar amount#

N starts as a price unit (cents, dollars, points). To turn it into "how much I lose," multiply by the contract's point value.

Kansas City wheat: one contract is 5,000 bushels, quoted in cents per bushel, so 1 cent = 5,000 × 0.01 = $50.

1N in dollars = 11.94 × 50 ≈ $597
2N ≈ $1,194

Corn: if N = 7 cents and one contract is 5,000 bushels, 1 cent = $50:

1N = 7 × 50 = $350
2N = $700

Same "one contract": wheat's 1N is $597, corn's is $350. That is why you cannot compare risk by counting contracts.

5. Turtle jargon: speak in N#

The Turtles did not think "I have a $1,000 bet on." They said, "I've got half an N on."

Erle Keefer explained: if you think "I have $34 million of bonds on," the money concept hits your lizard brain and you start saying "Oh my God." If you think "bonds moved one and a quarter N today," you are watching the market instead of your net worth.

Three benefits:

  • Comparable: one ruler across markets and contracts.
  • De-sensitising: converting dollars into volatility units reduces emotional interference.
  • Computable: size, stops and additions all express in N.

Daily life confirms it: each morning a Turtles received an envelope with their name on it, containing position printouts and updated N values. The team did the heavy arithmetic; the Turtles checked and executed.

6. N changes, and must be updated daily#

N is not a constant. Volatility shifts with sentiment, news and season. Eckhardt updated his estimates every day:

That's my routine. Two or three times a year I make an adjustment intra-day.

Therefore:

  • The same market can have very different N at different times. Low volatility, small N; high volatility, large N.
  • Small N → more contracts for the same risk; large N → fewer. That is the core of the next lesson.
  • Stop updating N and you are navigating with an old map.

7. Three uses of N (preview)#

  1. Position size: (2% of equity) ÷ (dollar value of 2N) = contracts.
  2. Stops: entry ± 2N is the initial stop.
  3. Pyramiding: add one unit for every 1N of favourable movement.

So N is not just a statistic; it is the system's reserve currency.

Key points#

  • N = 20-day average true range, the market's typical daily travel.
  • TR = max of (H−L), |prev close − H|, |prev close − L|.
  • Wheat example: 20 TRs sum to 238.75, so N = 11.94 cents.
  • N times point value gives dollar risk: wheat 1N ≈ $597, corn 1N = $350.
  • The Turtles spoke in N to avoid staring at dollar amounts.
  • N changes daily; it drives size, stops and pyramiding.

Self-test#

  1. A market closed at 100 yesterday; today's high is 106, low 97. What are the three distances and the TR?
  2. A contract has a point value of $20 and N = 3.5 points. What is 1N in dollars? 2N?
  3. Why can two markets with the same N carry very different risk when you trade the same number of contracts?
Answers

1. TR1 = 106 − 97 = 9; TR2 = |100 − 106| = 6; TR3 = |100 − 97| = 3. TR = max(9, 6, 3) = 9.

2. 1N = 3.5 × 20 = $70; 2N = $140.

3. "One contract" has a different point value. Equal N means equal price movement, not equal dollar movement; risk must be compared as N × point value.
