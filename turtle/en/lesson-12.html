Part Three · Lesson 12

Stops, Pyramiding and Cutting Back

About 7 min read·Based on Michael W. Covel's The Complete TurtleTrader

What you will learn: the complete Turtle exit system — the 2N hard stop, the ½N first-day stop, pyramiding, and how all stops move up with each new unit.

1. Two exits, whichever comes first#

The Turtles had two kinds of exit:

  1. The 2N hard stop: 2N against the entry. Touch it and you are out, no discussion.
  2. The S1/S2 opposite breakout exit: after a long, a new 10-day (S1) or 20-day (S2) low closes the trade.

The rule: whichever triggers first.

  • Immediate adverse move → the 2N stop takes you out with a small loss.
  • A big trend → the opposite breakout takes you out with a profit.

These cover two fates: small loss or large gain. The middle outcome — lose a little, win a little, then let it turn into a huge loss — is designed out.

Execution is mechanical: price touches, you exit. No reconsidering, no excuses. The book's example: corn entered at 250 with N = 7 cents, so 2N = 14 cents and the hard stop is 236. At 236 you are out.

One detail: on the first day of a position the stop can be tightened to ½N; from the next day, use 2N. That gives a new position closer protection at the cost of being stopped by ordinary noise. The book's pyramiding example starts directly at 2N to show the full process.

2. Why pyramid#

Trend following is extremely concentrated: most profit comes from a few large trends.

If you hold one unit when a trend begins, even a once-in-a-decade move pays you one unit's worth. The Turtles' solution was pyramiding: add to winners and make them big.

Rules:

  • Every 1N of favourable movement, add one unit.
  • Maximum 5 units.
  • Each new unit's stop is 2N against its own entry.
  • All earlier units' stops move up to the newest unit's stop.

That last rule is the key. Pyramiding does not simply pile on risk; it uses open profit to raise the whole position's stop. Late in the pyramid, the worst case often shifts from "a loss" to "a smaller gain."

3. The live cattle walkthrough#

The book's example: a $50,000 account, live cattle, $400 per point.

Unit one#

  • 1N = 0.80 points → 1N = 0.80 × 400 = $320
  • 2N = 1.60 points → 2N = $640
  • Allowed risk = 50,000 × 2% = $1,000
  • Contracts = 1,000 ÷ 640 = 1.56 → 1 contract
  • Entry 74.00, stop 74.00 − 1.60 = 72.40
  • Next add point = 74.00 + 0.80 = 74.80

Unit two#

Price reaches 74.80. Open profit 0.80 points = $320, equity $50,320.

  • Allowed risk = 50,320 × 2% = $1,006.40
  • Contracts = 1,006.40 ÷ 640 = 1.57 → 1 contract
  • Stops on both units move to 74.80 − 1.60 = 73.20
  • Next add point = 74.80 + 0.80 = 75.60

If both were stopped now: unit one +$320, unit two $0 — still a net gain of $320. The beginnings of locked profit.

Unit three#

Price reaches 75.60. Note: N has fallen to 0.70 (volatility shrank).

  • 1N = 0.70 × 400 = 280; 2N = **560**
  • Equity $50,960; allowed risk = $1,019.20
  • Contracts = 1,019.20 ÷ 560 = 1.82 → 1 contract
  • All three stops move to 75.60 − 1.40 = 74.20
  • Next add point = 75.60 + 0.70 = 76.30

Unit four#

Price reaches 76.30; N still 0.70.

  • Equity $51,800; allowed risk = $1,036
  • Contracts = 1,036 ÷ 560 = 1.85 → 1 contract
  • All stops move to 76.30 − 1.40 = 74.90
  • Next add point = 76.30 + 0.70 = 77.00

Unit five (the last)#

Price reaches 77.00. Note: N has risen to 0.85.

  • 1N = 0.85 × 400 = 340; 2N = **680**
  • Equity $52,920; allowed risk = $1,058.40
  • Contracts = 1,058.40 ÷ 680 = 1.55 → 1 contract
  • All five stops move to 77.00 − 1.70 = 75.30

Now the position is five contracts with one stop at 75.30. If everything were stopped there:

Unit Entry Stop Result ($400 per point)
1 74.00 75.30 +1.30 pts = +$520
2 74.80 75.30 +0.50 pts = +$200
3 75.60 75.30 −0.30 pts = −$120
4 76.30 75.30 −1.00 pts = −$400
5 77.00 75.30 −1.70 pts = −$680
Total −$480 (about −1%)

Five units fully loaded, worst case a 1% loss. That is the power of moving stops: risk did not grow linearly with position size; earlier open profit absorbed it.

The exit#

Live cattle rallies to 84.50, the exit criteria trigger, and everything is closed:

Unit Entry Exit Profit
1 74.00 84.50 10.50 pts = $4,200
2 74.80 84.50 9.70 pts = $3,880
3 75.60 84.50 8.90 pts = $3,560
4 76.30 84.50 8.20 pts = $3,280
5 77.00 84.50 7.50 pts = $3,000
Total $17,920 = 35.8% of original equity

One trade earns 35.8% while the worst case is a 1% loss. That is "cut losses, let profits run" in arithmetic form.

(One clarification: the book's "risk to original equity" column treats units already in profit as zero risk and counts only possible losses, giving 1, 200or2.4480 above.)

4. The cost of pyramiding#

It is not free:

  • It raises average cost: later units are bought higher, so a reversal gives back more.
  • It is dangerous in chop: without a trend, every "1N move" may be the last.
  • It is psychologically heavy: at five units, any pullback makes you want to run.

Hence three safety belts: the 2N stop, the ratcheting of all stops, and the five-unit cap.

5. Cutting back: the automatic brake#

Beyond exits, there is a portfolio rule (covered in Lesson 7):

For every 10% drawdown, cut unit risk by 20%.

2% → 1.6% → 1.28% → …

This is the mirror image of pyramiding:

  • When winning, add a unit every 1N — make winners bigger.
  • When losing, cut unit risk 20% per 10% drawdown — make losers smaller.

One enlarges winners, one shrinks losers. Almost all of trend-following money management compresses into those two sentences.

6. Turn this lesson into a checklist#

Before the open, ask in order:

  1. Where is my 2N stop? Has it been touched?
  2. Has an opposite breakout triggered an S1/S2 exit?
  3. Has price moved 1N in my favour? Should I add? After adding, have all stops moved up?
  4. What unit risk does current equity imply? Has a de-risking threshold been hit?
  5. Is this market already at the 4–5 unit cap?

If all five have clear answers, you do not need a "feeling."

Key points#

  • Two exits: the 2N hard stop or the S1/S2 opposite breakout, first to trigger.
  • A ½N stop may be used on day one; 2N thereafter.
  • Add one unit per 1N of favourable movement, up to five units.
  • Each addition moves all earlier stops to the newest unit's 2N stop.
  • The cattle example: $50,000 and one contract grows to five units and $17,920 of profit (35.8%).
  • At five units, a full stop-out can cost only about 1% because profit is locked in.
  • De-risking mirrors pyramiding: enlarge winners, shrink losers.

Self-test#

  1. A long entry at 100 with N = 4. Where is the 2N stop, and where is the first add point?
  2. After adding a third unit, price falls toward the second unit's entry. Where should your stop be?
  3. Why can a five-unit position lose less than the sum of five independent 2% risks?
Answers

1. 2N = 8, so the stop is 100 − 8 = 92; the first add point is 100 + 4 = 104.

2. Not at the second unit's entry. All stops sit at the newest unit's entry minus 2N. If the third unit entered at 108 with N = 4, every stop is 108 − 8 = 100.

3. Earlier units' stops have been raised above their entries, locking in profit. When price returns to the common stop, those gains offset the later units' losses, so net risk is far below the simple sum of 2% per unit.
