Part Three · Lesson 13

The Portfolio — Spread Your Bets Across Many Tables

About 6 min read·Based on Michael W. Covel's The Complete TurtleTrader

What you will learn: why the Turtles traded more than twenty markets, how to spot fake diversification, and how to compute net long/short units.

1. You do not know which table pays#

The hard truth of trend following: you cannot predict which market will trend big, or how far it will go.

This year may be the yen, next year crude, the year after cocoa. The only certainty is that a big trend will happen somewhere — you just do not know where.

Trade only three markets and the year's main move may be in the fourth; you end flat. Worse, the small losses along the way drain your capital and confidence before the real move arrives.

The Turtles' answer: cover as many loosely related markets as possible. If one of them trends, it pays for all the small losses elsewhere.

2. The original 21 markets#

The Turtles began with these:

Rates Currencies Commodities Index & energy
30-year T-bond Deutschmark Cotton S&P 500
10-year T-bond British pound Sugar Crude oil
90-day T-bill French franc Cocoa Heating oil
Eurodollar Japanese yen Coffee Unleaded gas
Canadian dollar Swiss franc
Gold
Silver
Copper

Twenty-one in total, spanning four different drivers: interest rates, currencies, agricultural/industrial commodities, and equity/energy.

The mix was not random. It was designed so that no single event would blow up every position at once.

3. Fake diversification: many names, one bet#

Suppose you hold four markets: corn, soybeans, gold, silver.

Four markets, four units, four opportunities — right?

  • Corn and soybeans are both grains, driven by weather, acreage and stocks. They move together often.
  • Gold and silver are both precious metals, driven by fear, the dollar and real rates. Highly correlated.

So those four "markets" contain about two independent sources of risk. You think you diversified; in reality you placed the same bet twice.

The book's analogies are direct:

  • The Dow and the S&P 500 are highly correlated. One unit of each is like two units of either.
  • Apple and Dell moved in lockstep in those years. One unit of each is double the Apple exposure.

Conclusion: correlated markets must be combined when counting risk, not counted separately.

4. More risk vs less risk#

Two portfolios with the same number of markets and the same number of units can carry very different risk:

More risk (highly correlated) Less risk (loosely correlated)
Longs Shorts Longs Shorts
Corn Gold Soybeans Gold
Soybeans Silver Japanese yen Five-year notes
Japanese yen Ten-year notes Live cattle Sugar
Five-year notes Crude oil

On the left: corn with soybeans, gold with silver, ten-year with five-year notes — four "independent" positions, really two or three risk sources.

On the right: soybeans, yen, cattle, sugar, crude, gold, five-year notes — different drivers. Historically the yen and crude oil do not move together.

Diversification is counted in independent risk sources, not market names.

5. Net long/short units: a counter-intuitive formula#

The Turtles had another trick: net long and short positions against each other.

Because longs and shorts often move oppositely in stress, holding both lets you carry more units without more risk.

The formula:

Net unit risk = larger side − (smaller side ÷ 2)

In other words: the smaller side counts at half weight.

Example one#

  • Long: corn, live cattle, cocoa, Swiss franc = 4 units
  • Short: wheat, sugar, ten-year notes = 3 units
Net risk = 4 − (3 ÷ 2) = 4 − 1.5 = 2.5 units

Eleven notional units, only 2.5 units of real risk.

Example two#

  • Long: corn (1), live cattle (3), cocoa (1), Swiss franc (2) = 7
  • Short: wheat (1), sugar (2), ten-year notes (1) = 3
Net risk = 7 − (3 ÷ 2) = 7 − 1.5 = 5.5 units

Example three#

  • Long: coffee (3), natural gas (1), soybeans (2), S&P 500 (2) = 8
  • Short: crude oil (4), Australian dollar (3) = 7
Net risk = 8 − (7 ÷ 2) = 8 − 3.5 = 4.5 units

This is why the Turtles looked over-leveraged to outsiders yet stayed inside their budget: notional exposure of 11, 10 or 15 units, net risk of only 2.5, 5.5 or 4.5.

6. Unit caps and portfolio caps#

Recall the per-market cap from Lesson 10:

  • One market: at most 4–5 units.
  • The whole portfolio: net units are used to keep total risk inside the budget.

If one unit is 2% of equity, 2.5 net units means 5% portfolio risk; 5.5 net units means 11%. The Turtles set an overall ceiling based on account size and risk tolerance.

Note: this formula is a heuristic, not a law of physics. It assumes longs and shorts really do offset in stress. In a liquidity crisis everything can fall together and the hedge fails. Use it as a supplement, never as a substitute for thinking about correlation.

7. The final order: practise#

At the end of the two weeks, the Turtles received not only rules but one overriding command: practise.

Two analogies from the book:

  • Tiger Woods: swinging a club on television at three years old, practising ever since. "No matter how good you get, you can always get better, and that's the exciting part."
  • Medical students: clumsy the first time they hunt for a vein, but through repetition and discipline they become doctors with decades-long careers.

Trading is the same. There is no secret sauce. One Turtle said it plainly:

Richard didn't quite give us the Holy Grail. There's no single magic element.

The rules take two weeks to teach. Execution takes a lifetime to practise.

Key points#

  • You cannot predict which market will trend, so cover many.
  • The original 21 markets spanned rates, currencies, commodities, equity and energy.
  • Correlation creates fake diversification: corn/soybeans, gold/silver, Dow/S&P are one bet twice.
  • Count independent risk sources, not market names.
  • Net units = larger side − (smaller side ÷ 2): 4v3 → 2.5; 7v3 → 5.5; 8v7 → 4.5.
  • Cap units at 4–5 per market; use net units for the portfolio.
  • Beyond the rules, the only command is practice.

Self-test#

  1. Six long units and two short units. What is net risk?
  2. Three long units and five short units. What is net risk?
  3. A portfolio holds one unit each of corn, soybeans, gold and silver. Roughly how many independent markets is that, and why?
Answers

1. 6 − (2 ÷ 2) = 5 units.

2. 5 − (3 ÷ 2) = 3.5 units (larger side minus half the smaller).

3. About two: corn and soybeans are highly correlated, as are gold and silver. Four notional units, two real risk sources — textbook fake diversification.
