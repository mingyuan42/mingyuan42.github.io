单位——一笔该做多大Units — How Big Is One Bet?

本课目标：掌握海龟的仓位公式，能对任意市场算出该做几手，并理解为什么“向下取整”和“单位上限”不是可选项。

一、单位：2% 的另一个名字#

海龟把“每笔交易愿意承担的风险”固定为账户权益的 2%，并把这么多风险称为一个单位（one unit）。

  • 10 万美元账户，1 个单位 = 2,000 美元风险。
  • 20 万美元账户，1 个单位 = 4,000 美元风险。

注意：单位不是一个固定的手数，而是一个固定的金额风险。 同样 2,000 美元的风险，在玉米上可能是 2 手，在日元上可能是 1 手，在债券上可能是 0 手。

这就是海龟能用两周培训去交易几十个完全不懂的市场的原因：他们不交易“玉米”或“黄金”，他们交易“单位”。

二、公式#

合约数 = (账户权益 × 单笔风险%) ÷ (止损距离对应的美元风险)

海龟的止损距离是 2N，所以：

合约数 = (权益 × 2%) ÷ (2N 的美元价值)

最后向下取整（2.67 → 2，1.40 → 1）。

三、玉米完整算例#

条件：

  • 账户权益：100,000 美元
  • 单笔风险：2%
  • 玉米 N = 7 美分
  • 玉米合约：5,000 蒲式耳，1 美分 = 50 美元

第一步，算 1N 的美元价值：

1N = 7 美分 × 50 美元/美分 = 350 美元

第二步，算 2N 止损的美元风险：

2N = 350 × 2 = 700 美元

第三步，算账户允许的风险：

100,000 × 2% = 2,000 美元

第四步，相除并向下取整：

2,000 ÷ 700 = 2.857… → 2 手

结论：这个突破信号出现时，买 2 手玉米。

不是 2.857 手，也不是四舍五入到 3 手。期货不能买小数手，而且向上取整会直接突破 2% 的风险预算。

四、同一套算法，换市场#

书中的对照表（每行的账户风险不同，重点看算法）：

市场 1N 美元 2N 美元 账户风险 应做手数
玉米 350 700 2,000 2.0
瘦肉猪 420 840 3,000 3.0
日元 725 1,500 1,875 1.0
十年期国债 525 1,050 2,000 1.0

再看两个完整练习：

例一：瑞士法郎

  • 权益 150,000，风险 1.5%
  • 1N = 800 美元，2N = 1,600 美元
  • 可承受风险 = 150,000 × 1.5% = 2,250 美元
  • 2,250 ÷ 1,600 = 1.40 → 1 手

例二：迷你玉米

  • 权益 25,000，风险 2%
  • 1N = 70 美元，止损用 3N = 210 美元
  • 可承受风险 = 25,000 × 2% = 500 美元
  • 500 ÷ 210 = 2.38 → 2 手

注意例二用的是 3N 止损。规则可以调整，但一旦定了，就必须用同一个止损距离去反推仓位，否则风险预算就是假的。

五、为什么必须向下取整#

因为风险预算是上限，不是目标。

  • 2.67 手向上取整成 3 手，实际 2N 风险变成 2,100 美元，超过 2,000 美元的预算 5%。看起来不多，但连续几十笔都超一点，就是系统性超支。
  • 向下取整到 2 手，实际风险 1,400 美元，只有 1.4%。少赚一点，但活着。

海龟的原则很朴素：宁可少做，不可超支。

六、反直觉的结论：波动越小，仓位越大#

公式里分母是 2N。所以：

  • N 小 → 分母小 → 手数多。
  • N 大 → 分母大 → 手数少。

这不是 bug，是设计。

书中有个大豆的例子：趋势启动时 N 较小，同样的账户可以买到的单位，是趋势末端 N 变大时的 2.5 倍。也就是说，行情刚启动时你仓位最大，行情走远、波动放大后你自动减仓。

杰里·帕克把这个特性变成了优势。他发现最好的趋势往往始于极低的波动：

如果近期波动很低——不是黄金 5 美元，而是黄金 2.50 美元——我们就会建一个非常大的仓位。

低波动通常意味着这个市场已经死了很久：所有人都讨厌它，一连串亏损，窄幅盘整。然后它轰隆隆穿过那些高点，我们就上车了。

这背后的逻辑是：低波动时建仓，止损很近，风险很小；一旦趋势展开，盈亏比极高。波动率不只是风险度量，它还是机会的价格。

七、单位上限：不许把鸡蛋堆在一个市场#

单位再合理，也不能无限加。海龟有一条硬约束：

任何一个市场，最多持有 4 到 5 个单位。

为什么？因为每个单位都是 2% 的风险。如果一个市场允许你加到 10 个单位，那这个市场单独就能让你亏掉 20%——这已经违背了“分散”的初衷。

同理，不同市场之间还有相关性限制（下一课讲）。单位上限和相关性上限合起来，构成了组合层面的风险预算。

八、一个必须跨过的理解门槛#

有位海龟这样描述初学时的困惑：

当有人说“N 是波动率，N 也是你的单位大小”，我会问：“我怎么分得清这两个？”这就像让你绕着一个谜团打转。但过一阵子就简单了。比如我对莉兹·谢瓦尔说：“我持了半个单位，赚了三个 N”，或者“我持了半个 N 的单位，浮盈半个 N”。我完全明白区别，根本不用想。这东西会烧进你的脑子。

这段困惑非常真实：

  • N 是价格单位：市场一天走多远。
  • 单位是风险单位：你愿意亏多少钱。
  • “赚了三个 N”说的是行情走了多远；“持了两个单位”说的是你下了多大的注。

分清这两件事，海龟系统就算真正入门了。

本课要点#

  • 一个单位 = 账户权益的 2% 风险，不是固定手数。
  • 合约数 =（权益 × 风险%）÷（止损距离的美元价值），海龟标准止损为 2N。
  • 玉米算例：2,000 ÷ 700 = 2.857 → 2 手；永远向下取整。
  • 同一算法适用于所有市场，所以不需要每个市场的基本面知识。
  • N 越小，仓位越大；低波动突破往往是最好的趋势起点。
  • 单市场单位上限 4–5 个，防止风险集中。
  • 区分“N（波动）”和“单位（下注大小）”。

自测#

  1. 账户 200,000，风险 2%，某市场 1N = 500 美元，用 2N 止损。应做几手？
  2. 账户 100,000，风险 2%，某市场 1N = 1,200 美元，用 2N 止损。算出结果后，你会怎么做？
  3. 为什么“向下取整”比“四舍五入”更符合风险管理的本质？
参考答案

1. 可承受风险 = 4,000；2N = 1,000；4,000 ÷ 1,000 = 4 手。

2. 可承受风险 = 2,000；2N = 2,400；2,000 ÷ 2,400 = 0.83 → 向下取整为 0 手。这意味着该市场波动太大，在 2% 预算内做不了一手，正确做法是**放弃这笔交易**，而不是硬做一手。

3. 风险预算是上限。向上取整会系统性突破上限，向下取整保证实际风险不超过计划；代价只是少赚一点，换来确定性。

What you will learn: the Turtle position-sizing formula, how to compute contracts in any market, and why rounding down and unit caps are not optional.

1. A unit is another name for 2%#

The Turtles fixed the risk per trade at 2% of account equity and called that amount one unit.

  • $100,000 account: one unit = $2,000 of risk.
  • $200,000 account: one unit = $4,000 of risk.

Note: a unit is not a fixed number of contracts; it is a fixed dollar risk. The same $2,000 might be two corn contracts, one yen contract, or zero bond contracts.

This is how two weeks of training could cover dozens of unfamiliar markets: the Turtles did not trade "corn" or "gold." They traded units.

2. The formula#

Contracts = (Equity × risk%) ÷ (dollar risk of the stop distance)

The Turtle stop distance is 2N, so:

Contracts = (Equity × 2%) ÷ (dollar value of 2N)

Then round down (2.67 → 2, 1.40 → 1).

3. A full corn example#

Inputs:

  • Equity: $100,000
  • Risk per trade: 2%
  • Corn N = 7 cents
  • Corn contract: 5,000 bushels, so 1 cent = $50

Step one, dollar value of 1N:

1N = 7 cents × $50 = $350

Step two, dollar risk of the 2N stop:

2N = 350 × 2 = $700

Step three, account risk allowed:

100,000 × 2% = $2,000

Step four, divide and round down:

2,000 ÷ 700 = 2.857… → 2 contracts

Conclusion: when that breakout fires, buy two corn contracts.

Not 2.857, and not rounded up to three. Futures do not trade in fractions, and rounding up breaks the 2% budget outright.

4. Same arithmetic, different market#

The book's comparison table (account risk differs by row; focus on the method):

Market 1N in $ 2N in $ Account risk Contracts
Corn 350 700 2,000 2.0
Lean hogs 420 840 3,000 3.0
Japanese yen 725 1,500 1,875 1.0
Ten-year notes 525 1,050 2,000 1.0

Two full exercises:

Example one: Swiss franc

  • Equity 150,000, risk 1.5%
  • 1N = $800, so 2N = $1,600
  • Allowed risk = 150,000 × 1.5% = $2,250
  • 2,250 ÷ 1,600 = 1.40 → 1 contract

Example two: mini corn

  • Equity 25,000, risk 2%
  • 1N = $70, using a 3N stop = $210
  • Allowed risk = 25,000 × 2% = $500
  • 500 ÷ 210 = 2.38 → 2 contracts

Example two uses a 3N stop. Rules can vary, but once the stop distance is set, size must be derived from that same distance, or the risk budget is fiction.

5. Why round down#

Because the risk budget is a ceiling, not a target.

  • Rounding 2.67 up to 3 makes the actual 2N risk $2,100 — 5% over the $2,000 budget. Small, but repeated across dozens of trades it becomes systematic overspending.
  • Rounding down to 2 leaves actual risk at $1,400, or 1.4%. Slightly less profit, still alive.

The principle is plain: better to trade less than to overspend.

6. The counter-intuitive result: lower volatility, larger position#

The denominator is 2N, so:

  • Small N → smaller denominator → more contracts.
  • Large N → larger denominator → fewer contracts.

This is a feature, not a bug.

The book's soybean example: at the start of a breakout, N was small enough that the position could be 2.5 times the size available at the end of the trend, when N had expanded. In other words, you are largest when the trend begins, and the system automatically shrinks you as volatility expands.

Jerry Parker turned this into an edge. He found that his best trends often began in very low volatility:

If the recent volatility is very low — not $5 in gold, but $2.50 in gold — then we're going to throw in a very large position.

When volatility is low, it usually means that the market has been dead for a while. Everyone hates the market, has had lots of losers in a row, tight consolidation. And then as it motors through those highs, we get on board.

The logic: entering in low volatility puts the stop close and the risk small; if a trend then unfolds, the payoff ratio is enormous. Volatility is not only a measure of risk; it is the price of opportunity.

7. Unit caps: do not pile eggs in one market#

However sound the unit math, you cannot add forever. The Turtles had a hard cap:

No more than four to five units in any one market.

Why? Each unit is 2% of risk. If a single market allowed ten units, that market alone could cost you 20% — which defeats diversification.

There were also correlation limits between markets (next lesson). Unit caps plus correlation caps together form the portfolio-level risk budget.

8. A threshold every beginner must cross#

One Turtle described the early confusion:

When somebody says, "N is volatility and N is your unit size," I say, "How do I know the difference between them?" It's really like wrapping yourself around a conundrum, but after a while it was easy. Pretend I am talking to Liz Cheval: "I have got a half unit on and I am three N up," or "I have got a half N unit on and I am half N positive." I totally understand the difference. This is burned into your brain.

The confusion is real:

  • N is a price unit: how far the market travels in a day.
  • A unit is a risk unit: how much you are willing to lose.
  • "Up three N" describes how far the market moved; "two units on" describes how big your bet is.

Separate those two and the Turtle system starts to make sense.

Key points#

  • One unit = 2% of equity in risk, not a fixed number of contracts.
  • Contracts = (equity × risk%) ÷ (dollar value of the stop distance), Turtle standard 2N.
  • Corn example: 2,000 ÷ 700 = 2.857 → 2 contracts; always round down.
  • The same arithmetic works in every market, so no fundamental expertise is needed.
  • Smaller N means a larger position; low-volatility breakouts are often the best starts.
  • Cap units at 4–5 per market to avoid concentration.
  • Keep "N (volatility)" and "unit (bet size)" distinct.

Self-test#

  1. Equity 200,000, risk 2%, 1N = $500, using a 2N stop. How many contracts?
  2. Equity 100,000, risk 2%, 1N = $1,200, using a 2N stop. After computing, what do you do?
  3. Why is rounding down more faithful to risk management than rounding to nearest?
Answers

1. Allowed risk = 4,000; 2N = 1,000; 4,000 ÷ 1,000 = 4 contracts.

2. Allowed risk = 2,000; 2N = 2,400; 2,000 ÷ 2,400 = 0.83 → 0 contracts. The market is too volatile for the 2% budget, so the correct action is to **skip the trade**, not force one contract.

3. The budget is a ceiling. Rounding up systematically breaches it; rounding down guarantees actual risk stays within plan, at the cost of slightly less profit.
