钱没有记忆Money Has No Memory

本课目标：把“无记忆”落到三件最容易出错的事上——盈利后的钱、亏损后的钱、以及你的入场价。

一、“这是赚来的钱，可以冒点险”#

有个想法听起来特别合理：本金是辛苦钱，要小心；用赚来的钱去冒险，输了也不心疼。

埃克哈特直接把这层心理安慰拆了：

这确实是个让人舒服的想法。输“他们的钱”当然不像输“自己的钱”那么难受。可是，钱以前属于谁，为什么重要？重要的是它现在属于谁（你），以及你现在该拿它怎么办。

假设你从 10 万做到 20 万。你不能说：“那多出来的 10 万，我可以拿去乱赌。”

为什么？因为那 10 万已经是你的钱。它和最初的 10 万没有任何区别：一样的购买力，一样的亏损痛感，一样的复利作用。把它当成“飞来横财”或“幸运钱”，只是在给自己发放一张违规许可证。

海龟被要求：对新增的 10 万，用和最初 10 万完全相同的谨慎与纪律。 五个问题没有变，只是答案里的数字变了。

二、约翰与玛丽#

埃克哈特喜欢用两个人做思想实验。

约翰和玛丽在其他所有方面都一样：同样的风险偏好、同样的系统。唯一区别是约翰的钱多 50%。后来约翰去南海滩度假，玛丽在这段时间赚了 50%。现在两人钱一样多。

问：他们接下来该怎么做？

答案：做完全一样的事。 钱是怎么来的、谁更辛苦、谁更聪明，都不相关。现在权益相同、系统相同、风险偏好相同，最优决策就相同。

再换一个版本：约翰比玛丽少 10% 的钱，但约翰先入场。等玛丽入场时，两人权益恰好相同。

埃克哈特说：一旦建仓完成，建仓价对后续决策不应该有任何影响。 他甚至要求海龟“像不知道自己的入场价那样”去交易。

这句话非常反直觉。我们本能地盯住成本价：

  • 赚了，就想“等回到成本价上方再走”；
  • 亏了，就想“等回本就走”；
  • 涨过成本价很多，就后悔“买少了”；
  • 跌破成本价，就拒绝承认“这笔已经错了”。

市场不知道你的成本价。它只按自己的供需走。你的入场价是记账信息，不是市场信息。

三、浮盈和已实现盈利，没有区别#

很多人有个执念：浮盈是“纸上的”，落袋才安心。于是赚一点就跑，生怕回吐。

埃克哈特的评价毫不客气：

未平仓权益和已平仓利润之间的区分，完全是空洞的。这是记账员的产物，对正确交易毫无意义。

为什么？因为不管你叫它浮盈还是已实现，它都是你此刻权益的一部分，都在承担同样的风险，也都有同样的机会成本。你因为“想锁定安全感”而提前离场，本质上是在用情绪替代规则。

而且这种心理会反向伤害你：它让你砍掉赢家、留住输家。 这正是趋势跟踪最致命的错误——趋势系统靠少数大赢家活着，你却把大赢家掐死在摇篮里。

四、市场复仇#

亏损之后，人最容易犯的错不是“继续亏”，而是“只想在这个市场里把钱赢回来”。

假设约翰在思科上亏了钱。这个市场“伤”了他。于是他不再问“现在最好的机会在哪里”，而是一心想着“我要在思科上把亏的赚回来”。他盯着思科，越盯越亏。

埃克哈特把这叫做**“市场复仇”**（market vendetta）。它的本质是：把市场当成了一个有针对性的人格，把交易当成了面子问题。

海龟被训练成“不可知论者”：不执着于哪个市场让你赚钱、哪个市场让你亏钱，只接受“哪个市场正在走出趋势”。

五、不要给亏损加仓#

亏损之后，还有一种更隐蔽的自我欺骗：

“我原来有 10 万的微软，现在只剩 9 万了，那我再投 1 万进去，因为现在便宜了。”

丹尼斯的比喻很传神：这就像一个孩子已经被热炉子烫过一次，却还要把手再放上去，只为了证明“错的确实是炉子”。

但要注意一个关键区别：因为亏损而加仓，和因为新信号而重新入场，是两件完全不同的事。

海龟止损后，如果系统再次给出入场信号，他们会毫不犹豫地重新进场——哪怕刚在这个市场亏过钱。区别在于：重新入场是因为信号，不是因为不甘心。

保罗·都铎·琼斯有个经典案例：他连续入场、被止损、再入场、再被止损，前后大约十次，每次亏 2%。第十一次，趋势终于展开，那一笔赚的钱不仅覆盖了前面所有假启动，还大赚一笔。但前提是：他每一次都严格照规则重新进场。

六、伯德的十五个空位#

拉里·伯德是历史上最好的三分射手之一，假设他平均命中率 40%。如果他突然连续投失 15 个，这意味着什么？

意味着他还是那个 40% 的射手，只是碰上了一段正常的连续失手。他不能因此停止出手。

棒球也一样：你连续十年打击率 .300，突然 25 个打数没有安打，你就不是 .300 打者了吗？不是。你还是得站上打击区，用你一贯的姿势挥棒。

这就是海龟被训练的统计心态：为长期赔率而下注，不为最近的结果而改动作。

丹尼斯还有一句更狠的话：

厌恶亏损的人，入错行了。

他的“秘密”不在于怎么处理赚钱的仓位，而在于怎么处理错误的仓位。把亏损控制住，才有资格等来正确的仓位。

七、不要等回调#

还有一个极常见的念头：信号来了，但价格已经冲出去了，等它回调到“便宜的位置”再买。

埃克哈特的回答是：没有统计依据支持这种等待。 如果大豆从 8 美元涨到 9 美元，就该在 9 美元买，而不是等它回到 8 美元——它可能永远不回来。

“入场价太高”这个担忧，在交易建立之后，会被证明远没有你想的那么重要。真正决定结果的，是你什么时候出场、仓位多大、亏损怎么处理。

本课要点#

  • 赚来的钱和自己的本金没有区别，不能用“反正是赚的”来放宽风险。
  • 权益相同、系统相同、风险偏好相同 → 决策必须相同（约翰与玛丽）。
  • 入场价是记账信息，不是市场信息；建仓后不应影响后续决策。
  • 浮盈与已实现盈利没有本质区别，为“锁定安全感”提前离场会砍掉赢家。
  • 不要“市场复仇”，不要因为亏损而加仓；但可以因为新信号重新入场。
  • 连败是统计常态，像伯德连续投失；照规则继续出手。
  • 不要等回调，信号出现就执行。

自测#

  1. 用“约翰与玛丽”的框架，解释为什么“用利润去冒险”在逻辑上站不住。
  2. “这笔我还差两个点就回本了，等回本我就走。”这句话违反了哪几条原则？
  3. 同样是“亏损后再次买入同一市场”，什么情况下是纪律，什么情况下是复仇？
参考答案

1. 利润一旦进入账户就成为权益的一部分。两个人若权益、系统、风险偏好相同，最优决策必须相同；把利润标记为“可以乱来的钱”，等于给相同权益设置了不同的风险规则，违背比较原则。

2. 违反了入场价无关原则（成本价不是市场信息）、无记忆原则（用过去的状态决定现在的动作），以及“浮盈/已实现无区别”的原则；本质是用情绪替代出场规则。

3. 如果重新买入是因为系统再次给出有效信号，且仓位、止损都按规则计算，那是纪律；如果是因为“想在这个市场把亏的赚回来”、无视信号而加仓摊平，那就是复仇，通常会扩大亏损。

What you will learn: how to apply "memory-less" thinking to the three places traders go wrong — money after profits, money after losses, and your entry price.

1. "It's house money — I can take more risk"#

It sounds sensible: principal is hard-earned and must be protected; profits are winnings, so losing them should not hurt.

Eckhardt dismantled the comfort:

It's certainly a comforting thought. It certainly can't be as bad to lose their money as yours. Can't it? Why should it matter whom the money used to belong to? What matters is whom it belongs to now (you) and what to do about it now.

Suppose you turn 100,000 into 200,000. You cannot say, "I can now take crazier risks with that extra 100,000."

Why not? Because the extra 100,000 is your money. It has the same purchasing power, the same pain on loss, the same compounding. Calling it "funny money" or "lucky money" is just issuing yourself a licence to break the rules.

The Turtles were taught to treat the new 100,000 with exactly the same care and discipline as the original. The five questions did not change; only the numbers in the answers did.

2. John and Mary#

Eckhardt liked a two-person thought experiment.

John and Mary are identical: same risk aversion, same system. The only difference is that John has 50% more money. John goes on vacation to South Beach; while he is away, Mary makes 50%. Now they have exactly the same amount.

What should each do next?

Exactly the same thing. How the money arrived, who worked harder, who is smarter — irrelevant. Same equity, same system, same risk aversion means the same optimal decision.

Another version: John has 10% less money but enters a trade first. By the time Mary enters, their equity is identical.

Eckhardt's conclusion: once an initiation is made, the initiation price should not matter at all to subsequent decisions. He wanted the Turtles to trade as though they did not know their entry price.

This is deeply counter-intuitive. We fixate on cost:

  • Winning, we think "I'll exit once it's back above my cost."
  • Losing, we think "I'll exit when I break even."
  • It ran far above cost, and we regret "I bought too little."
  • It fell below cost, and we refuse to admit "this trade is already wrong."

The market does not know your cost. It moves on supply and demand. Your entry price is accounting information, not market information.

3. Open equity and realised profit are the same thing#

Many traders believe paper profits are not real until booked, so they grab small gains before they evaporate.

Eckhardt was blunt:

The distinction between open trade equity and closed-out trading profits is completely vacuous. This is a bookkeeper's artifact. It has absolutely no relevance to correct trading.

Why? Because whether you call it open or realised, it is part of your equity now, carries the same risk and the same opportunity cost. Leaving early to "feel secure" substitutes emotion for rules.

Worse, the habit cuts both ways: it sells winners and keeps losers. That is fatal for trend following, which lives on a few large winners — winners you just strangled in the crib.

4. The market vendetta#

After a loss, the most common error is not losing more; it is wanting to win the money back in the same market.

Suppose John lost money in Cisco. That market "hurt" him. Instead of asking "where is the best opportunity now?", he thinks only "I have to get my money back in Cisco." He stares at Cisco, and the loss grows.

Eckhardt called this a market vendetta. It treats the market as a personality with a grudge, and trading as a matter of face.

The Turtles were trained to be agnostic: do not fixate on which market made or lost money; accept whichever market is trending.

5. Never add to a loser#

There is a subtler self-deception after a loss:

"I had 100,000 of Microsoft, now I have 90,000. I'll put in another 10,000 because it's cheap now."

Dennis's image is exact: this is the child who has already been burned by a hot stove, putting his hand back on it to prove the stove was wrong.

But note a crucial distinction: adding because you are losing is not the same as re-entering because of a new signal.

After a stop-out, if the system gives another entry signal, the Turtles took it without hesitation — even in a market that had just cost them money. The difference is whether you are acting on a signal or on resentment.

Paul Tudor Jones gave the classic example: he entered, was stopped out for 2%, re-entered, was stopped out again, roughly ten times in a row. On the eleventh, the trend took hold, and that one trade paid for all the false starts and more. But only because he re-entered by the rules every time.

6. Bird's fifteen misses#

Larry Bird was one of the greatest three-point shooters ever — say, a 40% shooter. If he suddenly missed fifteen in a row, what would that mean?

That he is still a 40% shooter going through a normal cold streak. He cannot stop shooting.

Baseball is the same: hit .300 for ten years, then go 0 for 25 — are you no longer a .300 hitter? No. You still walk to the plate and swing the way you always have.

That is the statistical mindset the Turtles were taught: bet the long-run odds, do not change your swing based on the last result.

Dennis put it harshly:

The trader who is averse to losses is in the wrong business.

His "secret" was not how he handled the right positions but how he handled the wrong ones. Controlling losses is what buys you the time to wait for the right ones.

7. Do not wait for a pullback#

One more common thought: the signal came, but price has already run. Wait for a cheaper pullback.

Eckhardt: there is no statistical basis for that wait. If soybeans go from $8.00 to $9.00, buy at $9.00 rather than wait for $8.00 — they may never come back.

The worry that "my entry is too high" will prove far less important once the trade is on than you imagine. What determines the outcome is when you exit, how big the position is, and how you handle losses.

Key points#

  • Profits are not "house money"; they are your equity and deserve the same discipline.
  • Same equity, system and risk aversion → identical decisions (John and Mary).
  • Entry price is accounting information, not market information.
  • Open and realised profit are economically identical; exiting for "security" kills winners.
  • No market vendettas, no averaging down; but re-enter freely on a fresh signal.
  • Losing streaks are statistical weather; keep swinging like a .300 hitter.
  • Do not wait for pullbacks; execute the signal.

Self-test#

  1. Using John and Mary, explain why "risk the profits" is logically incoherent.
  2. "I'm only two points from break-even — I'll exit when I get there." Which principles does this violate?
  3. Re-buying a market after a loss: when is it discipline, and when is it a vendetta?
Answers

1. Once in the account, profit is part of equity. If two traders have identical equity, system and risk aversion, their optimal decisions must be identical; labelling part of equity as "money I can gamble" applies different risk rules to identical equity, violating the comparison principle.

2. It violates the irrelevance of entry price (cost is not market information), memory-less trading (a past state dictating a present action), and the equivalence of open and realised profit. It replaces exit rules with emotion.

3. Discipline when a valid new signal fires and size and stop are computed by rule; vendetta when the motive is to win back the loss in that market, ignoring signals and averaging down — which usually enlarges the loss.
